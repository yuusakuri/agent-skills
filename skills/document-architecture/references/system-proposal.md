# System Proposal

Use this structure to support a customer or executive decision about investing in and introducing a system. Balance business value, technical credibility, delivery feasibility, cost, and risk.

## Inputs

- Customer context and verified problem evidence
- Desired business outcomes and decision criteria
- Current systems, constraints, stakeholders, and operating environment
- Candidate solution and architecture evidence
- Delivery assumptions, estimates, commercial conditions, and risks

## Standard structure

| Chapter | Purpose | Required content | Completion check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Executive summary | Enable a fast decision | Customer issue, recommendation, expected value, investment range, major risk, requested decision | A decision-maker understands the proposal on one page |
| 2. Background and customer challenges | Establish shared understanding | Current state, evidence, affected stakeholders, cost of inaction, why now | Claims are sourced and specific to the customer |
| 3. Goals and success measures | Define the outcome contract | Business outcomes, user outcomes, baseline, target, measurement owner and timing | Success can be measured after introduction |
| 4. Proposed system | Explain the solution in customer terms | Capabilities, user experience, major workflows, differentiators, fit to challenges | Each capability addresses a stated challenge |
| 5. Scope and exclusions | Bound delivery and price | Included organizations, processes, systems, data, locations, deliverables; exclusions and options | Estimate assumptions match the stated scope |
| 6. System structure and technical policy | Demonstrate feasibility | High-level architecture, integration, data, security, availability, hosting, technology rationale | Technical content is understandable and credible without becoming detailed design |
| 7. Introduction roadmap | Show how value will be delivered | Phases, milestones, validation points, dependencies, release or rollout approach | Early value and decision gates are visible |
| 8. Project organization and governance | Establish accountability | Customer and supplier roles, decision rights, meeting and escalation model, quality governance | Every critical decision and deliverable has an owner |
| 9. Migration, training, and operations support | Make adoption feasible | Data migration, cutover, training, change support, service desk, monitoring, maintenance | Introduction covers people and operations, not only software |
| 10. Schedule and cost | Present the commercial shape | Estimate basis, timeline, work breakdown, fees, licenses, infrastructure, recurring cost, payment assumptions | Numbers reconcile with scope and assumptions |
| 11. Benefits and return | Explain value and timing | Quantitative and qualitative benefits, calculation method, sensitivity, realization owner | Benefits are not presented as guaranteed without evidence |
| 12. Risks and conditions | Make uncertainty decision-ready | `RSK-*`, likelihood, impact, mitigation, owner, trigger; prerequisites and customer responsibilities | Material uncertainty is visible before approval |
| 13. Alternatives comparison | Show why this option is recommended | Status quo, smaller option, alternative approach, evaluation criteria, trade-offs | Recommendation follows from transparent criteria |
| 14. Decision and next actions | Convert interest into action | Requested approval, remaining due diligence, owners, dates, proposal validity | The next step is concrete and owned |

## Writing rules

- Lead with the customer's outcome and decision, not the supplier's technology.
- Separate verified facts, estimates, assumptions, options, and contractual commitments.
- Make cost and benefit calculations reproducible.
- Use ranges and sensitivity where uncertainty is material.
- Keep detailed requirements and design in referenced appendices or separate canonical documents.

## Required quality gates

- Scope, schedule, cost, organization, and benefits use the same assumptions.
- Every material customer responsibility and prerequisite is explicit.
- Technical claims map to an identified requirement or risk.
- The proposal ends with a clear decision request and next action.
